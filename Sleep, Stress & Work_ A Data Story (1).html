2. How to make your sleep better

Eight habits sleep experts commonly recommend. After you click “Compare me,” you’ll also get tips picked for you in the results above.

🕓Keep a steady scheduleSame bedtime and wake time every day, including weekends.
🌅Get daylight and moveMorning light and daytime activity help you fall asleep later. Avoid hard workouts right before bed.
☕Watch caffeine and alcoholSkip caffeine in the afternoon and evening. Alcohol can break up sleep later in the night.
🧘Wind down and manage stressTry 30 minutes of reading, stretching or slow breathing, or jot tomorrow’s to-dos so your mind can rest.
📱Put screens awayTurn off phones and TVs about an hour before bed.
🛏Make the bedroom sleep-friendlyDark, quiet and cool. Use the bed for sleep only.
🌙Work nights?Keep your sleep time as regular as you can, use blackout curtains, and limit caffeine late in your shift.
⏰Don’t lie awake stressingIf you can’t sleep after about 20 minutes, get up, do something calm in dim light, and return when sleepy.

When to get help: loud snoring, gasping or breathing pauses in sleep, or trouble sleeping for more than a few weeks that affects your day are worth discussing with a doctor.

6. What the data suggests

  • Job type matters. Short sleep ranged from about 31% (education, farming) to nearly 43% (production work) across major groups. Jobs with more shift work tend to rank high.
  • Night shifts stand out. Night-shift workers were far more likely to sleep 6 hours or less, especially in transportation (69.7%) and health care (52.3%).
  • Stress and sleep move together. Adults with high stress averaged 6.2 h of sleep vs. 7.1 h for low-stress adults, and Gallup found 63% of people who want more sleep feel frequent stress vs. 31% of those who get enough.
  • Age and education. Short sleep was lower for people 65 and older, and lower for college graduates than for people with some college.

7. Patterns vs. proof

  • Correlation is not causation. Stress and short sleep go together, but these surveys can’t show which causes which. APA notes the link likely runs both ways.
  • Groups, not individuals. A group average says little about any one person in that job.
  • Self-reported and dated. Most figures are self-reported. The CDC job data is from 2013–2014 in 29 states, so it may not reflect today or the whole country.
  • Different measures. Sources use different cut-offs (under 7 h, 6 h or less, 8 h) and different years, so compare within a chart, not across charts.
  • Hidden factors. Shift work, hours, age and income overlap with occupation.
